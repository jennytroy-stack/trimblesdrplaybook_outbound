PART I

The Outbound SDR Role

01Welcome to the Trimble AECO Outbound SDR Team

As an Outbound SDR, you play a critical role in helping organizations discover new ways to improve how they design, build, and operate the world's infrastructure. Every conversation you start has the potential to uncover a business challenge, introduce a new perspective, and create an opportunity that helps a customer transform how they work.

Inbound customers have already raised a hand. Outbound customers haven't — which means success here comes from initiative, research, and persistence rather than speed and triage. It takes curiosity, thoughtful preparation, personalized outreach, and the ability to build trust with people who don't yet realize there's a better way to solve their problem. (Section 2 gets into exactly how this differs from inbound.)

This playbook reflects how we sell at Trimble: the goal was never just booking meetings. It's creating business conversations that help customers solve problems they may not have fully named yet.

What This Playbook Covers

Over time, this guide will build out how to understand your role within the Trimble sales org, build territory and account plans, research target accounts and buying committees, develop industry expertise, run personalized multi-channel outreach, conduct real discovery, qualify opportunities, partner with your AE, use technology and AI well, and measure your own performance. It doesn't replace manager coaching, product training, or methodology courses — it's the practical layer that ties those together for day-to-day work.

Why Outbound Matters at Trimble

The most valuable opportunities aren't always the ones that come to us. Many organizations that would benefit most from Trimble's solutions haven't filled out a form or requested a demo — they're heads-down running projects, managing teams, and hitting deadlines. Outbound lets us reach these organizations before they've started a buying journey, surfacing opportunities to improve efficiency, reduce risk, and modernize how they work before a competitor gets there first.

Done well, this expands Trimble into new markets and accounts, builds relationships with future customers, and generates the qualified pipeline the business is forecasting against. Every new outbound conversation is a potential first chapter of a long-term customer relationship.

The Outbound SDR's Role in Revenue Creation

Rather than responding to existing demand, you're generating it — through research, outreach, and conversations that wouldn't have happened otherwise. That means holding several roles at once:

What Success Looks Like

Consistency in outreach matters, but it's not the differentiator — execution quality is. High-performing Outbound SDRs consistently build strategic account plans, prioritize the right opportunities, research before engaging, personalize across channels, ask insightful discovery questions, qualify effectively, hand off cleanly to their AE, keep CRM data accurate, and keep learning. Success is measured by the quality of the conversations and the pipeline they generate — not the number of activities logged.

What's Expected of You

Think strategically about your territory instead of working it reactively. Lead with curiosity instead of assumptions. Personalize every interaction — customers can tell when they haven't been researched. Execute with discipline; the fundamentals done consistently beat occasional bursts of activity. Collaborate as one team with your AE, fellow SDRs, and Marketing. Own your own development instead of waiting to be told what to improve. And put the customer first: before any outreach, conversation, or recommendation, ask yourself, "how can I create value for this customer?" Section 3 goes deep on the mindset behind each of these — this is just the preview.

Some of your conversations will turn into opportunities right away. Others will plant something that develops six months or a year from now. Either way, you're not just the first step in the sales process — you're the beginning of the customer relationship. Show up prepared, professional, and curious, and the relationships you build today become the pipeline Trimble runs on tomorrow.

02Understanding the Outbound Motion

Outbound customers aren't shopping for software — they're running their business, and many don't yet know a better way of working exists. That means your job isn't to schedule meetings; it's to create awareness, earn trust, and start conversations that otherwise never would have happened. It takes patience and preparation, and every interaction should create value whether or not it leads to a meeting this week.

Inbound vs. Outbound

Both motions build pipeline, but the mechanics are different enough that treating outbound like "inbound, but colder" doesn't work.

Inbound SDROutbound SDR
Responds to customer interestCreates customer interest
Customer has already engagedCustomer may have no prior engagement
Focuses on speed and qualificationFocuses on research and relationship building
Converts demand into opportunityGenerates demand through proactive outreach
Shorter path to discoveryLonger path to engagement

Neither motion outranks the other — together they produce a balanced, predictable pipeline.

Creating Demand, Not Manufacturing It

Creating demand means helping customers recognize an opportunity they hadn't fully considered — not convincing them they have a problem they don't. In practice, that's sharing a relevant industry insight, asking a genuinely good business question, or connecting an operational headache to a business outcome, with more curiosity than pitch. The interest comes from the conversation being valuable, not from a well-produced deck.

Pipeline Gets Built One Conversation at a Time

Every outbound opportunity moves through the same sequence: pick the right account, research the business, form a hypothesis about their priorities, identify the stakeholders, personalize the outreach, follow up consistently, run real discovery, qualify, and partner with your AE on the handoff. Skipping a step — usually research, in a rush to send the email — is the most common reason engagement is low.

Today's Buyer Has Already Done Homework

Before an SDR ever reaches them, most prospects have already researched potential solutions, compared vendors, read reviews, and talked to peers. That means you earn attention by adding something they don't already have — not by introducing yourself. Conversations land when they're relevant, personalized, timely, tied to business outcomes, and respectful of the person's time. Approach every interaction as helping, not selling.

Enterprise Buying Is a Team Sport

Trimble's Outbound SDRs support both Mid-Market and Enterprise New Logo AEs, where deals routinely involve executive sponsors, business leaders, operational users, technical evaluators, procurement, and finance. Don't assume one good conversation equals one decision. Work with your AE to map the broader buying committee and look for chances to engage more than one stakeholder over time.

Multi-Threading

Organizations decide collectively, and different stakeholders weigh different things. As you work an account, keep track of who actually experiences the problem, who owns the budget, who influences the decision, who will use the solution day to day, and who signs off on the investment. A single-threaded relationship — one contact, no matter how friendly — is the most common reason a promising opportunity stalls when that person goes quiet or changes roles.

Industry Expertise Changes the Conversation

Trimble's Outbound SDR org is aligned by industry — Civil, MEP, General Contracting, Structures, or ERP Cross-sell — specifically so reps can develop real fluency instead of generic messaging. Each of these has different priorities, workflows, technology ecosystems, buying triggers, and personas, and speaking that specific language is what makes an unsolicited email feel credible instead of like spam. As industry-specific playbook chapters get built out, they'll live alongside this one.

It's a Process, Not an Event

Very few opportunities come from a single email or call. A real outbound motion combines personalized emails, phone conversations, LinkedIn engagement, relevant follow-up content, account research, and ongoing coordination with your AE — sustained over time. Consistency beats intensity: professional persistence, always paired with something of value, wins more often than a flurry of activity in week one that fades by week three.

Three Questions Behind Every Good Outbound Motion

Why would this organization benefit from talking to Trimble? Who's most likely to care about the problem we solve? What insight or question can I offer that earns the right to keep the conversation going?

Keep these in view and outreach naturally gets more relevant and more consultative — the alternative is optimizing for volume and hoping something lands.

03The Outbound Mindset

Most days start with no queue waiting for you — no demo requests, no inbound inquiries. Every conversation begins with your initiative, which is exactly what makes the role both energizing and hard. Nobody succeeds here off one great email or one exceptional cold call; it's the mindset behind the daily work that actually compounds.

Curiosity Over Pitching

Customers don't want another pitch — they want someone who understands their business. That means listening more than you speak and learning before recommending. Swap "how do I convince them to meet with us?" for "what can I learn about their business that makes this conversation worth having?" Curiosity earns trust; trust is what creates the opportunity in the first place.

Consistency Beats Intensity

One exceptional day followed by a week of inconsistent effort doesn't produce predictable pipeline — daily fundamentals do: researching accounts every morning, completing the outreach you planned, following up when you said you would, keeping Salesforce current, reviewing your own calls. Small actions repeated consistently outperform occasional bursts every time.

Handling Rejection

Not every prospect responds, and not every conversation moves forward — that's the job, not a signal something's wrong with your approach. Often it has nothing to do with you: wrong timing, a budget freeze, a team mid-project, genuine busyness. Swap "they rejected me" for "the timing or circumstances weren't right," and treat every conversation as information that sharpens the next one.

Playing the Long Game

Enterprise and mid-market sales rarely happen fast. A conversation today might lead to a meeting six months out; a meeting this quarter might become an opportunity next year. Invest in the relationship, not just this week's outcome — persistence builds familiarity, familiarity builds credibility, and credibility is what eventually creates the opportunity.

Confidence Without Pressure

Customers respond to reps who are knowledgeable, prepared, and genuinely interested in helping — and disengage fast from anything that feels scripted or pushy. Confidence comes from preparation and product knowledge, not from controlling the conversation. Ask good questions, speak clearly, get comfortable with silence, and let the conversation move naturally. Your job is to invite a conversation, not force one.

Customer-First Prospecting

Every outreach should be able to answer one question honestly:

"Why should this customer spend time talking with me?"

Focus on business outcomes, industry challenges, and a genuinely relevant insight or question — and resist making it about Trimble too early. Customers engage when they feel understood, not marketed to.

Growth Mindset, Ownership, Resilience, Discipline

The strongest SDRs never assume they've mastered the role. A few habits show up consistently among the ones who keep improving:

TraitIn Practice
Growth MindsetWelcomes coaching, reviews their own calls, tries new techniques, learns from teammates
OwnershipBuilds their own account plans and prioritizes proactively instead of waiting to be told what's next
ResilienceAbsorbs missed calls, unanswered emails, and lost opportunities without letting them define the week
DisciplineProtects prospecting time and follows the plan even on low-motivation days

None of these are personality traits you either have or don't — they're habits, built the same way the daily-execution habits in "Consistency Beats Intensity" are built.

Bringing It Together

The strongest Outbound SDRs are curious before they're persuasive, consistent instead of sporadic, patient during long cycles, confident without being aggressive, and customer-focused rather than product-focused. None of that shows up as a single skill you either have or don't — it's a set of daily choices that compound, and it's the foundation everything else in this playbook builds on.

PART II

Understanding Trimble

04Understanding Trimble & the AECO Business

Customers rarely buy software because it has more features than the alternative — they invest because they're trying to solve a business problem, reduce risk, or get ahead of where their operations are headed. You don't need to become a product expert to be effective here. You need to understand Trimble, the industries we serve, and our customers' challenges well enough to create a conversation that's actually relevant to them.

Trimble's Mission

Trimble's mission is to transform the way the world works by connecting the physical and digital worlds — across construction, engineering, transportation, agriculture, geospatial, and utilities. Within AECO specifically, that means helping customers improve every phase of the built environment lifecycle, from planning and design through construction and operations. The underlying belief is simple: technology should help people work smarter, collaborate better, and get better results, not just digitize what they were already doing.

The AECO Business

Architecture, Engineering, Construction, and Owner & Operator organizations design, build, and maintain the infrastructure and buildings that support communities — often through projects involving multiple companies, hundreds of stakeholders, tight schedules, and real financial risk. Trimble supports that lifecycle with technology that helps teams plan more effectively, design with more accuracy, manage construction execution, connect field and office operations, and get real visibility into project performance. The pitch isn't a single-problem tool — it's connected solutions that improve how work gets done end to end.

The Industries You Might Support

Trimble's Outbound org is organized around five industry segments — Civil, MEP, General Contracting, Structures, and ERP Cross-Sell — each with its own priorities, personas, and buying triggers. Section 5 is the full reference for whichever one you're assigned to; this chapter stays at the company level.

The Construction Technology Landscape

Construction has historically run on manual processes and disconnected systems, and that's changing as projects get bigger and more complex — organizations are investing in BIM, project management, estimating, field operations, equipment management, scheduling, and business intelligence, often specifically to close the gaps between departments and project phases. Much of this sits under the broader banner of digital transformation: not just buying new software, but changing how the organization operates in response to labor shortages, rising material costs, tighter schedules, and margin pressure. It's worth understanding as a trend, not a single event — it gives you a shared vocabulary with customers who are already thinking about it, even if they haven't started evaluating vendors.

Why Customers Buy Trimble

Every customer is different, but the outcomes they're chasing tend to cluster into a handful of themes:

OutcomeWhat It Looks Like

VisibilityLeaders get real access to project, operational, and financial information

Outcomes Over Features

The single most useful shift you can make in how you talk to prospects: stop leading with what the software does, and start leading with what organizations like theirs are trying to accomplish. "Our solution includes…" invites a polite nod. "Organizations like yours often use Trimble to…" invites a real answer. Section 6 goes deep on how to apply this to specific product conversations — this is the underlying principle everything there is built on.

Your Role

You're not expected to have every answer. You're expected to understand the industries you support, recognize the challenges that come up again and again, do enough research to ask a sharp question, and know when to bring in your AE. Think of yourself as the first chapter of the customer's relationship with Trimble — the quality of that first conversation is what earns the right to a second one.

05Industry Specialization

Relevance is what makes a cold outreach land. A Civil contractor, an MEP firm, a General Contractor, a Structures shop, and an existing ERP customer all sit inside the same broader construction ecosystem, but their priorities, workflows, and technology needs are genuinely different — which means one message doesn't work for all five. You don't need to be a technical expert in every solution area to specialize well. You need enough knowledge of your assigned industry to personalize outreach, ask sharper discovery questions, recognize a real buying signal, and partner effectively with your AE. The goal isn't to tell customers what their challenges are — it's to ask questions informed enough that they want to answer them.

Civil

Civil organizations design, build, and maintain infrastructure — roads, bridges, airports, rail, utilities, land development — ranging from regional contractors to enterprises running complex, multi-site infrastructure programs. Their work depends on coordination between office teams, field crews, equipment operators, and other stakeholders spread across locations.

What they're wrestling with: productivity (completing projects faster, better equipment utilization, less downtime), project complexity (coordinating across many teams and changing site conditions), accuracy and rework (errors in planning or communication turning into costly delays), and field-to-office collaboration (getting information moving fast enough for good decisions).

PersonaTypically Cares About
Executive Leadership (CEO, President, Owner, VP Operations)Growth, profitability, competitive advantage, operational efficiency
Operations Leaders (Director of Ops, VP Construction)Productivity, standardization, project performance, resource management
Project ManagersSchedule management, communication, coordination, risk reduction
Field Leaders (Superintendents, Foremen)Execution, visibility, equipment performance, productivity

Buying triggers to watch for: winning a larger project than usual, expansion into a new market, headcount or equipment growth, visible operational inefficiency, or a stated digital transformation initiative.

Discovery starting points: "How is your team measuring productivity today?" "How connected are your field and office teams?" "What processes require the most manual effort right now?" "How are you preparing your operations to scale?"

MEP (Mechanical, Electrical & Plumbing)

MEP organizations design, fabricate, install, and maintain the building systems that have to be accurately coordinated and delivered inside a larger construction project. Common friction points: coordinating complex designs, managing project changes, fabrication efficiency, installation issues, and labor constraints. Priorities tend to cluster around prefabrication, workflow standardization, digital design processes, and reducing rework.

Messaging guidance: lead with coordination, efficiency, reduced project risk, and predictability — not features. "How is your team coordinating design changes across trades today?" opens more doors than a list of capabilities.

General Contracting

GCs coordinate the full project across subcontractors, owners, and other stakeholders, with roles spanning executives, project executives, PMs, estimators, superintendents, and VDC teams. Their priorities: on-time delivery, cost control, communication, risk reduction, and project visibility. Watch for technology trends they're already investing in — digital project management, mobile field tools, and integration across systems — since those signal where a Trimble conversation might fit naturally.

Structures

Structures organizations focus on structural design, detailing, fabrication, and construction, typically balancing precision requirements against the need to deliver efficiently. Recurring problems: design coordination, fabrication accuracy, production efficiency, and reducing errors and rework. Buying motions are often triggered by business growth, a new project win, or a stated push toward operational modernization. Stakeholders usually include owners, engineering leaders, fabrication managers, and project leaders.

ERP Cross-Sell

This is different from the other four — these are existing Trimble customers, and the job is expansion, not net-new acquisition. Look for signals like business growth, new capabilities being added, disconnected systems that are visibly causing pain, or a push for more operational visibility. The framing matters here more than anywhere else: the question isn't "what else can we sell this customer," it's "where can Trimble create more value for a business we already partner with." That means understanding their current environment first — current challenges, existing Trimble usage, future goals — and it means real coordination with the account owner, Customer Success, and product teams before you reach out. Nobody wants a cross-sell pitch from someone who doesn't know what they already own.

Building Your Industry Knowledge

Specialization isn't a one-time onboarding task — it builds through customer conversations, AE feedback, Gong call reviews, and just paying attention to what comes up repeatedly. Every conversation is a chance to learn something that makes the next one better. As deeper industry-specific playbooks get built out (target profiles, messaging by persona, objection handling, customer stories), they'll extend this chapter rather than replace it.

PART III

Territory & Account Planning

07Territory Ownership

Great Outbound SDRs don't treat their territory as a list of accounts to work through — they treat it as a business they're responsible for growing. That's a shift from a task-based question ("what accounts do I need to contact today?") to a strategic one ("where are the best opportunities to create value for customers and build pipeline for Trimble?"). Strong SDRs don't wait for opportunity to appear. They actively create it.

What Territory Ownership Means

Owning a territory means becoming the expert on your assigned market: which companies are in it, which align with Trimble's ICP, which represent the biggest opportunity, what challenges those organizations likely face, who the key stakeholders are, and what events might create urgency. None of this is static — companies grow, leadership changes, projects start, priorities shift. The job includes continuously updating your read on the territory, not just building it once.

Balancing Two Time Horizons

A territory strategist holds two things at once: short-term pipeline creation (engaging active prospects, following up on buying signals, booking discovery calls) and long-term market development (building relationships with strategic accounts and future buyers that won't pay off this quarter). Favoring only one leaves either your pipeline or your future pipeline thin.

Understanding the Landscape You Own

Before executing outreach, get oriented on three things. Market composition — how many target accounts you have, which industry segments and company sizes they span, and where Trimble already has a presence. Account landscape — which accounts are strategic, which are growing, which are existing customers, and which represent whitespace (Section 9 covers whitespace analysis in depth). Industry trends — market shifts, technology adoption patterns, and industry events that give you a relevant reason to reach out.

Building a Territory Plan

Without a plan, SDRs default to reacting instead of strategically creating pipeline. A plan should answer who you're targeting and why, who the key contacts are, what challenges might create an opening, how you'll engage, and how you'll measure progress. The mechanics of account tiering and prioritization live in Section 8; the mechanics of building a plan for a specific strategic account live in Section 9. This chapter is about the territory-level view that sits above both.

Coverage vs. Depth

A recurring tension: more accounts contacted, or deeper engagement with fewer? The honest answer is both, applied selectively — broad coverage is how you find opportunity, and deep engagement is what turns a promising account into a qualified one. Enterprise accounts in particular need more research, more stakeholders, and more persistence than a quick email allows. Strategic accounts should get strategic effort; not every account deserves the same investment.

Keeping the Territory Current

Review your territory on a regular cadence, not just when something breaks — new accounts appear, markets shift, and engagement levels change. Protect real time for research and planning so your day doesn't become entirely reactive. Every interaction generates intelligence worth capturing: a customer priority, an organizational change, a technology insight. And periodically ask what's actually converting, which messages are landing, and where the patterns are — territory strategy should update based on what you're learning, not stay fixed from the plan you wrote in January.

The difference shows up in the question you ask yourself each morning. A reactive SDR asks "who should I call next?" A territory owner asks "which conversation will create the greatest value for this customer and for Trimble?"

The Golden Rule of Territory

Unless you're specifically told otherwise, you're only allowed to reach out to accounts that are in your AE's name — this applies to any Account record that already exists in Salesforce DX.

For a company that isn't in DX yet, you can call it if you have good reason to believe it's actually in your territory and GAR range — don't call blind on a hope. If you get on the phone and realize partway through that the company is outside your territory, don't just drop it: do your best to still book the meeting and hand it to the appropriate AE. You'll get credit for that.

If you book an SAO on an account that already exists in DX but isn't in your AE's name, that credit can be withheld. Confirm ownership before you invest real time in an account, not after you've booked the meeting.

08Target Accounts & Ideal Customer Profile (ICP)

More activity doesn't automatically create more pipeline — the quality of the accounts you pursue shapes the quality of the opportunities you create. A strategic SDR doesn't ask how many accounts they can contact today; they ask which accounts have the greatest potential to actually benefit from a conversation with Trimble. Account selection is where good outbound strategy starts.

ICP vs. Buyer Persona

These answer two different questions, and conflating them is a common early mistake.

Ideal Customer ProfileBuyer Persona
Answers"Which companies should we target?""Who within that company should we engage?"
ExamplesIndustry, company size, revenue, geography, project complexity, technology environmentExecutive leaders, operations teams, project managers, technical users, finance stakeholders

The right account with the wrong contact stalls. The right contact at the wrong account caps your upside. You need both to line up.

What Makes an Account a Fit

When evaluating a potential target, weigh industry alignment (does it sit in Civil, MEP, GC, Structures, or ERP Cross-Sell, where Trimble has real expertise?), company size and complexity (does the scale of the operation make a technology investment meaningful?), operational complexity (multiple teams, multiple sites, and field-office coordination usually mean more room for Trimble to help), and growth potential (expanding teams, new markets, and bigger projects tend to create new technology needs).

Great SDRs don't wait for a customer to announce they're buying — they watch for signals that a relevant need or window of opportunity exists.

Account Tiering

Not every account deserves the same investment. A simple three-tier structure keeps that decision consistent:

TierCharacteristicsApproach
Tier 1 — StrategicStrong ICP fit, enterprise potential, real executive engagement possibleHighly personalized, multi-threaded, long-term relationship investment
Tier 2 — High PotentialGood ICP fit, clear business need, room to growPersonalized outreach, targeted messaging, consistent cadence

How to Prioritize Within a Tier

Five factors, applied consistently, keep prioritization from becoming a gut call: fit (ICP alignment), pain (evidence of a challenge Trimble can address), timing (a reason this account might be open now), access (can you actually reach the relevant stakeholders), and potential (does this represent real business value if it converts). A small share of accounts in any territory will score well across all five — that's where the majority of your strategic effort should go. The goal isn't maximum coverage; it's maximum impact.

09Building Account Plans

More calls, emails, and LinkedIn messages don't automatically create better results — the highest-performing SDRs do the thinking before the first outreach, not after. That's the real shift in this role: from find contacts → send messages → schedule meetings to understand the business → identify opportunities → engage stakeholders → create conversations → build pipeline. An account plan is what makes that shift concrete — a strategic view of a target account that helps you and your AE agree on why it matters, who's involved, and how you'll engage over time.

This matters more in Mid-Market and Enterprise selling specifically because these deals rarely move through one conversation with one person. A plan is what turns "I found a contact" into "I understand the organization and have a strategy for the right people."

What's In a Plan

Account overview, organizational structure, stakeholder map, business priorities, technology landscape, relevant trigger events, an engagement strategy, and an AE partnership plan. Not every account needs all of this in full depth — Tier 1 accounts (Section 8) warrant the complete version; Tier 3 accounts warrant a lighter pass.

Mapping the Account Hierarchy

Large organizations rarely have one entry point. A single business unit can have its own priorities, its own technology needs, and its own decision-makers — so before reaching out, get a rough picture of the company's structure: business units, regional teams, operating groups, and who owns strategic decisions versus operational execution. A simple version might look like: executive leadership at the top, business leadership below it (VP Operations, VP Construction, VP Technology, VP Engineering), and functional users below that (PMs, superintendents, estimators, field teams). The point isn't a perfect org chart — it's knowing where in that structure your conversation is most likely to be relevant.

Buying Roles: Who's Who in the Decision

Not every contact plays the same role. Section 2 already covers the basic case for multi-threading; here's the taxonomy for identifying who's who once you're inside an account.

White-Space Analysis

White space is the opportunity sitting inside your territory or an existing account where Trimble has limited or no engagement — a department not using Trimble, a new business unit, a stakeholder nobody's talked to yet. It matters because the easy mistake in outbound is only working accounts that already feel familiar; real growth often comes from the parts of an account nobody's developed. Work through it in four passes: the existing relationship (what does this account already use, and who's engaged), business units (new locations, divisions, or expanding teams), workflows (where are there gaps or disconnected handoffs), and stakeholder coverage (who do we know, and just as important, who don't we know yet).

Installed Technology & the Competitive Landscape

Customers rarely evaluate technology in a vacuum — they already have platforms, workflows, and preferences that shape how a new conversation lands. Understand what they're currently running, who else they might be evaluating or already using, and where the current setup is visibly limiting them (manual work, duplicate processes, no integration). Good discovery questions here: "What systems are currently part of this workflow?" "How well do your tools work together today?" "What would you improve about your current process?"

Trigger Events, Applied

Section 8 covers the full taxonomy of buying signals and trigger events. Once you've spotted one for a specific account, the job here is connecting it to your plan: what does this trigger imply about who's newly relevant, what conversation it opens, and how soon you should act on it before the window closes.

Multi-Threading in Practice

Start with one entry point — a relevant challenge and an initial contact. Expand deliberately from there to other leaders, users, and technical stakeholders. Then connect the conversations: look for where an operations leader's efficiency concern, a project leader's visibility concern, a technology leader's integration concern, and an executive's growth concern actually point at the same underlying opportunity. That alignment across roles is usually what turns a single relationship into a real opportunity.

Partnering With Your AE: The 30/60/90

You understand the company (industry, business model, priorities, growth signals), the people (economic buyer, champion, influencers, users), the opportunity (business challenges, technology landscape, trigger events, potential value), and you have an actual engagement strategy (messaging, channels, timeline, AE alignment). If any of those four are blank, the plan isn't ready yet — more research beats a rushed first touch.

10Account Research

Research is the difference between "I wanted to introduce Trimble and see if you're interested in learning more" and "I noticed your organization is expanding its infrastructure project portfolio — many companies experiencing similar growth are looking for ways to improve visibility across teams. I was curious how your team is approaching that today." Same intent, completely different odds of a reply. The gap is insight, and insight comes from research done with a purpose, not research done for its own sake.

Quality Over Quantity

The goal isn't to gather everything knowable about an account — it's to find what actually improves the conversation. Before you spend time on a data point, ask: will this help me have a better conversation? If the honest answer is no, it's probably not worth the time, no matter how interesting it is.

PART IV

Prospecting Excellence

11Building an Outbound Strategy

Outbound success doesn't come from sending more emails or making more calls — it comes from strategy applied consistently. Every account is a unique situation: a different organization, different people, different challenges, and a different reason a conversation would actually be worth having. The goal was never to contact people. It's to create relationships that lead somewhere.

From Contacts to Accounts

Traditional prospecting thinks in contacts: find someone, send an email, make a call. Strategic outbound thinks in accounts: understand the business, identify the opportunity, engage the right stakeholders. That shift matters most for Enterprise and Mid-Market accounts, where a decision rarely rests with one person and relationship-building takes real time.

Account-Based Prospecting

This is the discipline of prioritizing specific accounts and building a deliberate engagement strategy around each one, instead of reaching broadly at anyone who might be interested. In practice it's four steps: identify the opportunity (why this account, why now, why Trimble — the same test from Section 10, applied at the strategy level), define a hypothesis for why they might care, identify who actually experiences the problem versus who owns the budget versus who'd benefit from a fix, and build the engagement plan — messaging, channels, timing, and how your AE fits in. None of this works as a one-time exercise; it's a loop that tightens as discovery confirms or corrects your hypothesis.

Persona Mapping

Different roles inside the same account care about different outcomes, which means your messaging can't be one-size-fits-all. Section 13 has the full persona-by-persona messaging guidance — the short version is that executives tend to care about growth and strategic positioning, operations leaders about efficiency and standardization, project leaders about schedule and coordination, and technical users about workflow and integration. Knowing which persona you're talking to should shape the conversation before you ever open your mouth.

Contact Prioritization

This is a different axis from the account tiering in Section 8 — that's about which accounts deserve the most investment; this is about which contacts within an account deserve the most effort. Weigh influence (how much impact does this person have on the decision), relevance (do they actually experience the problem you're addressing), access (can you realistically build a relationship with them), and timing (is there a reason they'd be open to this now).

Not every touch needs the same depth of personalization, and very few opportunities come from a single interaction. 12Outbound Messaging

Message quality determines whether a prospect sees your outreach as valuable or as noise. Most sales messages get ignored because they're focused on the seller — what we offer, what we do, why we're different. Effective outbound messaging starts somewhere else entirely: the customer's business, their challenges, and the outcome they're after. The goal was never to sell a product in an email. It's to create enough relevance and curiosity for a real conversation.

Three Questions Every Message Should Answer

Why you — why are you reaching out to this specific person? Why this company — why does this organization make sense as a prospect? Why now — what makes this moment relevant? (This is the same test as the Why Them / Why Now / Why Trimble framework in Section 10, just applied to the message itself rather than the account decision.) A strong message connects customer context, business challenge, and potential value into an actual invitation to talk:

"I noticed your team has been expanding its project portfolio across multiple locations. Organizations experiencing similar growth often look for ways to improve visibility and consistency across operations. I was curious how your team is approaching that today."

Value Over Features

Section 6 already makes the case for leading with outcomes instead of capabilities — this is that principle applied to a single message. Compare feature-based ("Trimble offers software that helps manage construction workflows") to value-based ("Many construction organizations are looking for ways to improve visibility across teams and reduce the friction created by disconnected workflows. I was curious how your organization is approaching that today"). The second one gives the reader something to react to.

A simple formula gets you there: state an observation ("I noticed your company recently expanded into additional markets"), connect it to likely business impact ("organizations growing quickly often face challenges maintaining consistency across teams"), and close with a conversation starter ("how is your team approaching that today?").

Personalization Levels

Not every touch needs the same depth of research behind it — match the effort to the level of personalization you're aiming for.

Email Frameworks

Problem → Insight → Question is the simplest structure for creating curiosity: name a common challenge, share an observation about this account, invite conversation. Trigger-based outreach works when you have a specific reason to engage: reference the trigger, explain why it matters, invite the conversation. Executive outreach should skip product details and technical language entirely and stay at the level of growth, strategy, and efficiency.

Across all three: keep it more "you" than "we," keep it short enough to read in ten seconds, and make the ask a conversation, not a demo request. "Would it be worth comparing notes?" earns more replies than "can I show you a demo?" this early.

Call Structure

A cold call isn't meant to close anything — it's meant to create engagement. Structure: introduce yourself and ask permission ("this is an unexpected call, do you have 30 seconds for why I'm reaching out?"), connect to relevance (why you're calling this account specifically), ask a discovery question that invites real dialogue, listen for challenges and priorities, and if there's alignment, propose a concrete next step.

Voicemail Structure

A voicemail's job isn't to deliver your full message — it's to create recognition and a reason to call back. Introduction, reason for calling, one line of value, and a call to action ("I'll send a quick email as well"). Keep it under 20 seconds.

LinkedIn Messaging

LinkedIn outreach should read like relationship-building, not a sales email with a different header. A connection request stays simple and low-pressure; the follow-up after connecting references what you do generally and asks a genuine question about their priorities. Engage with their posts before you ask for anything — familiarity built passively makes the first direct message land better.

13Discovery in Outbound

Outbound prospects didn't raise their hand — which means your first job isn't to qualify interest, it's to create it. Section 2 already covers how outbound differs from inbound at the motion level; the difference that matters here is narrower: inbound discovery usually starts from an existing challenge the prospect already recognizes, while outbound discovery often has to first establish whether a challenge exists worth talking about at all. The goal isn't to sell. It's to uncover.

Earning the Conversation

Prospects don't take a meeting because an SDR wants time on their calendar — they take it because they believe the conversation might be worth something. Three things earn that: relevance (why does this topic matter to this company specifically), curiosity (giving them a genuine reason to think, not just react), and value (making it worthwhile even if nothing comes of it today). Approach every call with "I believe there may be an opportunity to improve something, and I want to understand how you're handling it today" rather than "I already know what your problem is."

Discovery Questions That Actually Open Up a Conversation

Keep questions open-ended and business-focused rather than yes/no. Understand the current state ("How is your team managing this today?" "What's working well?"), surface the challenge ("Where do you see the biggest opportunity for improvement?" "Where does your team experience friction?"), get to impact ("How does that affect your team?" "What happens when that issue comes up?"), and check priority ("Is this something your team is actively focused on right now?").

Business Pain, By Type

Pain is the gap between where the customer is and where they want to be — and it shows up in a few recognizable flavors: operational (inefficient workflows, manual processes, poor visibility), financial (rising costs, shrinking margins, lost productivity), strategic (trouble scaling, competitive pressure, unmet digital transformation goals), and people (labor shortages, training gaps, growth outpacing the team). Naming which type you're hearing helps you connect it to the right kind of business impact later in the conversation.

Qualifying Without Checking Boxes

Outbound qualification isn't a checklist — it's confirming there's a real opportunity worth an AE's time. Four things to establish: need (is there a challenge Trimble might help with), impact (does it actually affect the business), priority (is it important enough to act on), and timing (is there a reason to evaluate change now). You're identifying the challenge, the current process, the desired improvement, the relevant stakeholders, and the level of interest — you're not fully diagnosing their environment, positioning every product detail, or doing the AE's discovery for them. You create the opportunity. The AE deepens it.

Handing Off Without Making Them Start Over

The prospect should feel like the next conversation builds on this one, not that they're repeating themselves. Before transitioning to an AE, capture customer context (company, industry, situation), the business challenge and why it matters, the current state and tools in use, the desired outcome, who was involved in the conversation, and why now matters.

The difference between a handoff that works and one that doesn't is entirely about specificity:

Weak: "They are interested in learning more."
Strong: "This company is expanding its project portfolio and is looking for ways to improve visibility across teams. They currently manage this process manually, and the operations team identified improving efficiency as a priority. They'd like to explore what options exist."

Set the expectation for the prospect too — something like "Based on what you shared, it'd make sense to connect you with [AE], who works with organizations like yours and can dig into your goals in more detail." That one sentence is often what prevents a no-show.

14Objection Handling

Outbound means starting conversations with people who weren't necessarily looking for one, so objections are baked into the job — "we're already using something," "we're too busy," "send me information," "we're not looking," "call me next year." None of these automatically mean there's no opportunity. More often they mean the value of the conversation isn't clear yet, the timing feels off, or the prospect is protecting their time from what looks like a generic pitch. Section 3 covers the mindset for handling rejection in general; this chapter is the tactical layer — what to actually say when a specific objection comes up.

Stay Curious, Not Defensive

The instinctive move is to immediately counter the objection, which usually starts a debate instead of a conversation:

Prospect: "We're already using another platform."
Weak: "Actually, Trimble is better because…"
Better: "That makes sense. Many organizations we speak with already have processes in place. I'm curious, how is your current approach working for your team today?"

A Framework for Any Objection

Acknowledge ("that makes sense," "I appreciate you sharing that") without agreeing there's no opportunity — it's about respect, not concession. Clarify what's actually behind the objection ("can I ask what led you to that?"). Explore whether a real business challenge or opportunity exists ("are there any areas you'd like to improve?"). Advance to whatever's appropriate — continued discovery, an AE conversation, a resource, or a professional close if there's genuinely no fit right now.

"We're already using something."Satisfied, or switching feels like too much effort"That makes sense. How is that working for your team today?" Then: what do they like most, and is there anything they wish worked better?
"We're too busy."Competing priorities, or the value isn't clear yet"Completely understand. Out of curiosity, what are the biggest priorities your team's focused on right now?"

Staying Level-Headed

When a prospect pushes back, resist the urge to get defensive, talk faster, or over-explain — pause, listen, and ask instead. Remember they may be mid-crisis, managing competing priorities, or simply skeptical because they field a lot of sales calls. A calm, curious response changes the direction of more conversations than a well-rehearsed rebuttal ever will. If you catch yourself reaching for "we can help with that" or "our solution is different," swap in a question instead — "how are you approaching that today?" or "what's worked well with your current approach?" almost always opens more than it closes.

When to Push and When to Let Go

Keep going when the prospect shares a real challenge, shows curiosity, or timing seems flexible. Step back when they explicitly ask not to be contacted or the conversation clearly isn't going anywhere productive. Professionalism in the moment you let go is what keeps the door open for later.

Before responding to any objection, a quick gut-check: did I acknowledge what they said, stay curious instead of defensive, ask a real follow-up, understand what's actually behind it, and land on a clear next step either way? An objection isn't the end of a conversation — it's usually the start of understanding what the customer actually thinks.

15Multi-Channel Prospecting

Buyers don't engage with sales outreach in one place — an email, a LinkedIn interaction, a voicemail, an event conversation, a colleague's mention all build awareness in different ways. The mistake is treating each channel as its own disconnected activity. Every touch, across every channel, should reinforce the same underlying message: I understand your business, I think there may be an opportunity worth discussing, and I'd value hearing how you're approaching it today.

Why More Than One Channel

Senior leaders and operational teams are already flooded with communication, so a single email is easy to miss — a coordinated combination is more likely to land. Different people also just prefer different formats: some respond to a call, others to a sharp written insight, others to a peer recommendation. And for Mid-Market and Enterprise deals specifically, multiple stakeholders and longer timelines mean the familiarity you build early compounds into something real later.

The Channels

Section 12 already covers how to write for phone, email, LinkedIn, and voicemail — this is about when and why to reach for each one, plus a few channels that don't come up as often.

Phone remains the fastest way to create real dialogue — email communicates, but a conversation builds a relationship. Email gives you an asynchronous way to provide context and keep something moving between calls. LinkedIn builds familiarity before you ever ask for time — engage with a prospect's content before messaging them directly. Video is worth the extra effort for strategic accounts, executive outreach, or a follow-up after some engagement already exists; keep it short, personalized, and centered on one clear next step. Events (conferences, webinars, trade shows) create relationship-building moments before, during, and after the event itself — identify target accounts ahead of time and follow up with something specific afterward. Referrals from existing customers, AEs, or partners carry built-in trust; make the ask easy by giving your connector a clear reason and clear value to pass along. Social engagement — commenting thoughtfully, sharing relevant insight — builds credibility passively; the mindset shift is asking "how do I build relationships through this" rather than "how do I sell through this."

How the Channels Work Together

A coordinated sequence for a strategic account might look like: email on day 1 introducing a relevant challenge, a call on day 2 referencing it, LinkedIn connection and engagement on day 3, a call plus voicemail on day 5, a resource-sharing email on day 7, a personalized video on day 10 for strategic accounts specifically, and a final check-in around day 14 on timing and priorities. The specific cadence matters less than the principle: each touch should add something, not just repeat the ask.

For a true Enterprise account, the channel mix often varies by stakeholder even within the same sequence — an executive contact might get a personalized email, LinkedIn engagement, and a video message, while an operations leader gets a phone conversation and an industry-insight email, and a technical user gets workflow-specific messaging and a resource. The account-level message stays connected even as the channel and angle shift by persona.

Matching Effort to Account Tier

Strategic (Tier 1) accounts warrant deep research, custom messaging, multi-threaded engagement, and close AE collaboration. High-potential (Tier 2) accounts warrant persona-based messaging and consistent, research-backed outreach. Broader prospecting relies more on efficient workflows and strong default messaging frameworks than on deep customization per account. (Section 8 has the full account-tiering breakdown.)

PART V

Partnering with Sellers

16SDR & AE Partnership

An SDR can't create meaningful pipeline alone, and an AE can't effectively work an account without the intelligence and early relationship-building an SDR provides. The relationship works best under shared ownership — shared accounts, shared strategy, shared accountability — rather than a relay-race handoff where "the SDR books the meeting and the AE takes over." That framing creates gaps. The stronger version: the SDR and AE work together to create, develop, and advance opportunities, starting well before any meeting gets booked, during account planning itself (Section 9).

Who Owns What

The SDR owns territory intelligence (target accounts, industry trends, customer signals), account engagement (outreach strategy, prospect conversations, stakeholder relationships), pipeline creation (new conversations, qualified opportunities), and surfacing customer insight back to the team. The AE owns account strategy (industry expertise, opportunity strategy), customer engagement once a conversation is underway (discovery, solution alignment), opportunity progression (evaluation, business case, next steps), and coaching the SDR on messaging and prospecting. Both jointly own territory success, account prioritization, pipeline creation, and the customer's overall experience — those aren't things either role delivers alone.

Planning the Territory Together

A territory isn't just a list of accounts — it's a shared business opportunity, and SDR/AE alignment on it should cover the territory overview (segments, geography, market opportunity), account priorities (what needs immediate focus vs. nurturing vs. more research), coverage strategy (who owns engaging which accounts and personas), and shared success measures (pipeline goals, activity expectations, conversion targets). Section 7 covers how an SDR thinks about territory solo; this is the version done jointly with your AE.

Weekly Planning

Without a regular cadence, SDR and AE work drifts apart even with the best intentions. A weekly planning conversation should cover: territory review (priority accounts, new signals, any strategy shifts), prospecting focus (who's being targeted, which personas, what messaging), pipeline creation (new meetings, opportunities identified), blockers (difficult accounts, competitive intel, resources needed), and clear next actions with owners attached. Fifteen minutes of real alignment here saves hours of working at cross-purposes later.

Selecting Accounts Together

Section 8 already covers the account-selection criteria (fit, pain, timing, access, potential) in full — use that same framework here rather than building a separate one. What's different in this context is that the decision should be made jointly: an SDR's read on ICP fit and buying signals paired with an AE's read on strategic value and long-term relationship potential produces a sharper prioritization than either working alone.

Joint Prospecting

Some of the strongest engagement comes from SDR and AE working an account together rather than in sequence. In executive outreach, the SDR identifies the target and handles research and outreach execution while the AE contributes industry credibility and an executive-level perspective to the messaging. In a strategic account campaign, the SDR maps stakeholders and builds the engagement plan while the AE shapes account strategy and supports customer conversations as they start. Around events, the SDR identifies targets and coordinates outreach beforehand, both build relationships during, and both follow up after — nobody's chasing a lead alone that the other already has context on.

Feedback Runs Both Ways

SDRs are positioned to notice prospect reactions, market signals, and which messaging is actually landing — that's valuable input for the AE and for refining outreach. AEs are positioned to notice meeting quality, discovery outcomes, and qualification gaps — equally valuable input back to the SDR. Good feedback is specific and actionable, not a verdict. "The meeting wasn't good" teaches nothing; "the prospect was interested, but we needed more detail on business impact before advancing" gives the SDR something to actually adjust.

Pipeline Reviews Are Strategy Sessions, Not Status Checks

An SDR should come ready to discuss which accounts are being targeted, what's been created, and where the friction is. An AE should come ready to share what's working in the opportunities that have advanced and what context would help going forward. The real questions worth asking together: are we engaging the right accounts and the right personas, is there a genuine business challenge and urgency behind what's in the pipeline, and what should change about where we're focusing next.

Shared Accountability, In Practice

Neither "the AE owns the opportunity now" nor "the SDR just needs to create more meetings" reflects how this actually works well. The better question, asked by both sides: what can we do together to create a better outcome here? Partnerships that operate this way tend to track three things together — activity (accounts engaged, conversations created), quality (meeting quality, conversion, pipeline contribution), and collaboration itself (planning consistency, feedback exchange, strategy alignment).

Rules of Engagement With AEs

The problem this exists to avoid: SDRs sending an AE a hundred DX links asking "can I reach out to this person?" Assuming the account is already in your territory (Section 7), a simple test decides whether you need to ask first:

  • No open opportunity on the account? Call anyone. If you notice your AE reached out recently, or has a future task logged against a record on that account, it's your call whether to give them a heads-up — you don't have to ask permission.
  • Open opportunity on the account? Ask the AE first. The COO might be mid-cycle on ProjectSight, but that doesn't mean the Controller isn't a legitimate ERP conversation — the AE just needs to be looped in before you start it.
  • Opportunity Closed Won in the last 6 months? Ask the AE first. There are plenty of good reasons not to pitch a new product onto an account that just bought something — let the AE make that call.

Rules of Engagement With Other SDRs

If an Inbound SDR is actively working Prospect A at a company, an Outbound SDR is free to work Prospect B at that same company — whoever books the meeting first gets the SAO credit. What you don't do is reach out to a prospect a colleague is already actively working; check their Gong record before you start a sequence to make sure you're not stepping on an active thread.

Who Owns the Outreach

This is a team game, but ownership does shift at a predictable point. Before the Intro Call, it's the SDR's: calendar invitations, reschedules, pre-meeting follow-up, sending the Google Meet link and agenda. After the Intro Call, if the opportunity moves to Discovery stage, the AE owns any and all follow-up outreach. If it doesn't SQO, it goes back to being a team effort — SDR and AE both work to salvage it, and the SDR shouldn't be shy about taking the reins if that's what moves it forward.

17Opportunity Qualification & Handoffs

An outbound program isn't measured by meetings booked — it's measured by whether those meetings turn into conversations an AE can actually build on. A strong handoff creates momentum; a weak one creates friction and makes the customer feel like they're explaining themselves from scratch. The standard to hold: the prospect should feel like Trimble already understands their business, not like they're repeating themselves to a new person.

Qualification Is Understanding, Not Gatekeeping

The question isn't whether a prospect is "good enough" — it's whether there's a real business challenge, a reason to explore change, and genuine alignment between what they need and what Trimble does. Section 13 already covers the core qualification lens (need, impact, priority, timing); add two more dimensions here that matter specifically at the handoff stage: authority (who's actually involved — stakeholders, decision-makers, influencers, users) and current environment (what technology and workflows exist today, and where they're falling short). The SDR isn't expected to complete full discovery, design a solution, or build a business case — that's the AE's job. The SDR creates the opportunity; the AE develops it.

What Makes an SAO Real

Mechanically, an SAO is created the moment you create the Opportunity record for a booked meeting — the record lands in the "Prospecting / Pursue" or "Qualifying / Validating" stage. But that mechanic only fires after the meeting clears the quality bar: an actual relevant conversation with a potential business challenge behind it, not just a calendar invite a prospect agreed to. A quality SAO captures customer context (company, industry, role), the business challenge itself, their current state, the outcome they want, and why the timing makes sense now. If any of those five are missing, don't create the Opportunity yet — the meeting isn't there.

An SQO is an SAO that's actually moving — typically defined by hard next steps already on the calendar. To get SQO credit, the Opportunity record has to advance to the "Discovery" stage or beyond; a meeting that happened but didn't produce a next step doesn't count yet, however good the conversation felt.

Crediting across motions: if a prospect an Outbound SDR was working comes in through an inbound channel within 30 days of the last outreach, the SAO credit stays with the Outbound SDR. Outside that 30-day window, credit goes to the Inbound SDR who picks it up.

What Belongs in Salesforce

At minimum: account information (company, industry, territory, ownership), contact information (name, role, persona), a conversation summary (why they engaged, what was discussed, the business challenge and any relevant initiatives), qualification notes (need, impact, stakeholders, timing, current state), and clear next steps (meeting date, AE involved, what the customer expects). The gap between a useless note and a useful one is almost entirely specificity:

Weak: "Interested in Trimble. Wants a demo."
Better: "Operations Director at ABC Construction is evaluating ways to improve visibility between field and office teams as project volume increases. Current process relies on disconnected systems, creating reporting challenges. Interested in understanding options for improving workflow efficiency. AE conversation scheduled to explore current processes and priorities."

Preparing for the Meeting

Before the AE conversation, an SDR should have researched the account (background, industry, recent initiatives, growth signals) and the contact (role, responsibilities, priorities), formed a hypothesis about likely challenges and relevant discovery questions, and aligned with the AE directly on meeting goals, customer background, and what questions are worth exploring. This isn't a formality — it's what lets the AE walk in already several steps into the conversation instead of starting cold.

Setting the Customer's Expectations

Tell the customer why the meeting is happening, who'll be there, and what's worth expecting from it:

"Based on our conversation, I think it would be valuable to connect you with [AE Name], who works with organizations like yours. They can learn more about your current processes, understand your goals, and discuss where Trimble may be able to support your team."

That sets up what the customer should actually expect: a conversation that clearly builds on what's already been discussed, framed around discovery rather than a pitch, and handled professionally enough that they feel understood rather than processed. A fuller version of the same idea, said out loud on the call before you send the invite:

"Tom, while I'm sending you this calendar invitation I want to make sure we're on the same page. Next Thursday, you'll meet my colleague, Phil. It'll be a standard Discovery Call where we will both learn what's going on in each other's businesses and understand if we could be a good fit for each other. If it goes well, we typically move forward with scheduling a demo for you to see any platforms you might be interested in. Is that fair?"

Setting the bar this explicitly — this is a real Discovery conversation, not a quick 15-minute favor — is what increases both show rate and SQO rate. A prospect who agreed to a vague "quick call" has an easy out. One who agreed to what you just described doesn't.

Booking the Meeting: Step by Step

  1. Create and send the calendar invitation.
  2. Add the description to the invitation (see the template below) — but wait until you're off the phone to do it, so you can get the invite itself into the prospect's inbox as fast as possible while you're still live with them.
  3. Create the Opportunity record.
  4. Remove every other prospect at that company from cold outreach in Gong.

Create the Opportunity when the prospect actually wants to meet — waiting until there's a defined time on the calendar is typically the right call, rather than creating it the moment interest is expressed.

Calendar Invitation Standards

Title: "Trimble | [Prospective Company Name]: Intro Call." Length: 30 minutes. Attendees: the appropriate AE, you, the prospect, and anyone else from their organization the prospect wants to include. Product Sellers are not scheduled onto Intro Calls by default — if you think the prospect would benefit from one, flag it to your AE and let them make that call. Always include a Google Meet link.

Description template:

"[Prospect Name], on this call we'll discuss: [three things they were interested in on the cold call]

There will be plenty of time for you to ask us some questions and understand how we can improve your business together.

Here is my cell phone: [number]. If something pops up, let me know and we can reschedule.

See you there!"

Stay on the phone until the prospect actually receives and accepts the invite — ask them to accept it while you're still talking. If the invite doesn't seem to be landing, tell them you'll stay on the line, since sometimes it just needs a moment to come through.

The Handoff, Phase by Phase

Before the meeting: update Salesforce, share context with the AE directly (not just through notes), and align on goals. During the meeting: introduce context if it's useful, support the transition, and keep capturing anything new that comes up. After the meeting: review the outcome together with the AE, update next steps, and agree on the follow-up strategy. None of these phases is optional if the goal is a handoff that actually holds up.

Before creating an SAO, the fast check: is it clear why this conversation matters, what business challenge is behind it, what the AE needs to know walking in, and whether the customer will feel understood rather than repeated to? A meeting was never the outcome. A meaningful conversation is — and a good handoff is what turns that into real pipeline.

Tricks to Increase SAO → SQO Rates

  1. Set proper expectations on the cold call. Let the prospect know exactly what they're signing up for — this isn't a short, low-stakes chat. It's an Intro Call: they're agreeing they have a problem worth exploring.
  2. Book the Intro Call as soon as possible. Time kills deals — keep the gap between the cold call and the Intro Call as short as it can be.
  3. Send the calendar invite while you're still on the call.
  4. Tell the prospect you need to stay on the line in case the invite doesn't go through right away — it gives you a reason to stay engaged until it's confirmed.
  5. Send a short, concise follow-up email with genuinely relevant information — pull a resource from Seismic, and introduce your AE by name, ideally with the AE replying to say hello.
  6. Send a reminder the day before and the morning of the Intro Call.

The Ultimate Goal: Running Strategic Plays

So what do you do if your AE sends you a list of accounts in Salesforce with a G-Chat message saying "go for it!"? Your immediate reaction shouldn't be to start blindly cold-calling. To protect your efficiency and find the highest-probability buyers, systematically narrow that list down so only your absolute "good fits" remain — with one goal in mind: running highly targeted sales plays.

What is a "sales play"? At Trimble, a sales play is a repeatable, coordinated outreach strategy — combining phone calls, custom emails, and LinkedIn touches — triggered by a specific business event, tech stack signal, or customer scenario. A play is the opposite of generic, "spray-and-pray" prospecting: it lets you approach a specific persona, speak directly to the pain point they're facing today, and present a tailored value proposition that feels warm, timely, and hyper-relevant.

Layering the Three Elements of a "Good Fit"

  • Good-fit accounts: Filter the AE's list against your target criteria — are they in your territory, and do they match the employee size, Gross Annual Revenue (GAR), and NAICS codes for your ICP? (If unsure what a good account looks like, reverse-engineer recent closed-won accounts.)
  • Good-fit contacts: Filter for the precise buying-committee personas with the right titles. For Civil, that's VP of Operations, Survey Manager, Chief Estimator, GPS Coordinators.
  • Good-fit contact info: Filter results to only show contacts with verified direct-dial phone numbers and business emails.

Building the intent search: select topics (broad bundles like Cloud ERP, or narrower ones like Construction Accounting Software), filter by industry and territory to keep the list clean, and set the date range — last 30 days for a broader view, last 7 days for the hottest signals.

Customer Expansion tip: if a customer rep recently sold Tekla to a Mid-Market or Enterprise account, pull that account into the intent panel and check whether they're researching ERP or financial suites. Construction ERP migrations are massive, high-dollar deals — catching an existing customer researching accounting software is the wedge to introduce Trimble Construction One before a competitor gets in.

Drilling down: select the whole page of spiking companies, click "Open in Advanced Search," pivot to the Contacts tab, and filter for the actual decision-makers (Estimators, CEOs, CFOs). Save the search and check it every morning to catch territory accounts heating up in real time.

Executing on intent: these accounts need a different operational playbook — don't drop them into a standard 30-day sequence. Run a compressed, high-velocity flow (more dials, shorter gaps) to book the meeting before the buying window closes. If a few weeks of high-velocity outreach gets zero response, pull them out and drop them into a long-term nurture instead of burning more energy.

The golden rule of intent messaging: don't be creepy. Never reveal your source.

Creepy: "Hey, I saw you guys were looking at ERP Solutions online, so I wanted to call…"

Strategic: "Hey John, a lot of CFOs in the heavy civil space tell us that as they scale, their biggest bottleneck is manual data re-entry between estimating and back-office accounting. I know operations visibility is a major focus for teams like yours right now. How are you currently bridging the gap between the field and your financial systems?"

Use the intent data to form a strategic assumption and write as if it's a happy coincidence — hit the exact problem they're solving for without making them feel watched.

How Do You Know You're Targeting the Right Accounts?

Judge a book by its cover. High-yield prospecting requires execution viability before you ever pick up the phone — visually inspect company names, target titles, and website projects/imagery to confirm real-world fit against your ICP. And audit data density: a seemingly perfect Tier 1 account with no direct dials or verified emails is a data ghost town an SDR can't actually break into. A "data-rich" account — dense, clean contact data across multiple personas — is always the premium target.

Once found, mine the profile: recent posts (proof you did your homework), career history (did they come from a Trimble customer? trigger the Champion Tracking play), and mutual connections or shared groups for rapport.

Parting Thought: Data Cleansing Is Prospecting

It's easy to treat data filtering, list cleaning, and account mapping as administrative overhead — a chore before the "real work." Flip that: the meticulous effort spent fixing, filtering, and refining your database right now is what saves you hundreds of wasted dials and dead ends later. Clean data is the high-leverage foundation pipeline gets built on.

Make it a non-negotiable habit to fill the gaps — hunt down missing names within the account hierarchy, map the buying committee to exact validated titles, and lock in direct dials and verified emails before a contact ever touches a sequence.

Data is a guide, not absolute truth. No platform is perfect — algorithms occasionally misclassify accounts or surface an outdated title. Don't let rigid parameters box you in. Treat the tech stack like a sandbox: experiment with filters, run keyword experiments, and adapt based on what's actually working on the phones. The elite SDRs don't just follow the data blindly — they use it as a launchpad to hunt creatively.

22New Logo SDR Plays

Seven repeatable plays for finding and engaging net-new logo opportunities.

1. Closed-Lost Re-engagement

Just because a company said "no" or "not right now" 6–12 months ago doesn't mean it's a permanent no.

Trigger: A deal that went Closed-Lost 6+ months ago due to "Timing," "Budget," or "Lost to Competitor."
Strategy: Research what's changed since the last conversation — a new decision-maker, new funding, a product release that solves the exact reason they dropped out.

"When we spoke last year, [Previous Pain Point] was a roadblock, and the timing wasn't right. I'm reaching out because we just launched [New Feature/Solution] which specifically solves that, and I wanted to see if priorities have shifted for 2026."

Building your personal Closed-Lost playbook: open the master shell template (SFDX Closed Lost Report — Shell for SDRs), click "Save As" to create your own editable version, then tailor the filters — update Opportunity Owner Names to your current AEs, your name, and any previous SDRs who worked your territory, and adjust the Close Date filter to isolate accounts that went cold 6–12 months ago, the re-engagement sweet spot.

Look closely at the "Lost Reason" notes on old deals. A prospect who suffered "severe software fatigue" or a "budget freeze" last year is a great candidate for a fresh, workflow-driven conversation today.

2. Champion Tracking

The lowest-hanging fruit in outbound: a buyer who already loves Trimble but just switched jerseys.

Trigger: A power-user, champion, or economic buyer from an existing customer leaves their company and takes a job at a new logo account.
Strategy: Use LinkedIn Sales Navigator alerts on "Past Company" filters. When a champion moves to a non-customer company, target them immediately — they already know, trust, and love the product.

"Congrats on the new role at [New Company]! I loved working with you over at [Old Company] to help scale their operations. I'd love to sync for 15 minutes to show you what we're working on now, and see if we can help you make a quick impact in your first 90 days here."

3. Executive Job Changes

A new VP or C-level hire usually comes with a mandate for transformation and fresh budget allocation.

Trigger: A decision-maker persona (VP of Operations, CIO) has been in role less than 90 days (via Sales Nav or ZoomInfo Scoops).
Strategy: Reach out during the onboarding window (days 30–90), when they're actively auditing existing processes and looking for modern tools to prove their value to the board.

"Congrats on taking the reins of the Operations team. Typically, when new leadership steps into a role like yours, mapping out data silos and field efficiency is top of mind. Here is how we helped [Similar Peer Company] optimize their workflow during a similar transition…"

4. Competitor Contract Window Play

Time your outreach to hit right as a competitor's contract comes up for renewal.

Trigger: ZoomInfo technographic data shows a target account using a direct competitor, combined with manual research suggesting they've had that tool roughly 1–2 years (standard contract length).
Strategy: Start multi-threading 90–120 days before the suspected renewal date — waiting until 30 days out means they're already locked in.

"I see you are currently utilizing [Competitor] for your fleet management. A lot of companies we talk to switching over to us are experiencing [Common Competitor Flaw/Pain Point]. Since you might be evaluating your stack ahead of your next renewal cycle, I wanted to share…"

5. Intent Data Play

Instead of guessing who's in-market, look at who's actively researching your category.

Trigger: A target account shows a spike in intent data (ZoomInfo or Bombora) for keywords like "BIM software compliance" or "telematics integration."
Strategy: Don't say "I saw you searching for us." Treat the data as a smoke signal — if there's smoke, there's fire. Drop 3–4 personas from that company into a highly relevant, problem-centric sequence immediately.

"A lot of [Job Titles] I speak with right now are struggling to bridge the gap between their field hardware data and office SaaS metrics. We actually just put together a framework on how to solve this…"

6. Reverse-Engineering a Good Account From Closed-Won Opportunities

Look at who already gave Trimble money, figure out why, and go find their lookalikes.

  • Closed-Won audit: extract the DNA of 3–5 accounts in your territory that closed in the last 6 months — size (revenue/headcount), exact vertical niche (heavy highway vs. utility), and current tech stack.
  • "Good account" checklist: a high-probability civil account usually has high asset density (lots of heavy equipment), an active public bid profile (winning DOT or infrastructure contracts), and a "digital gap" (strong office engineers but manual paper/stakes in the field). Look for NAICS codes 237310 (Highway, Street, and Bridge) or 238910 (Site Preparation).
  • Clone them: in ZoomInfo, take your closed-won list and hit "Generate Lookalikes." In Sales Navigator, go to a successful customer's company page, scroll to "Similar Companies," and harvest the 5–10 peer accounts LinkedIn suggests.

7. Marketing Prioritized Leads (MPLs) Play

MPLs represent demonstrated interest — a webinar attendee, a whitepaper download. This play is about speed-to-lead: striking while the iron is hot, before warm inbound intent cools down.

Trigger: A high-intent MPL is generated from marketing engagement and assigned to you.
Strategy: Speed and consistent tracking are the priority. You have a strict 1-business-day window to make first touch, which secures an exclusive 3-day ownership protection window. Before logging any outreach, officially claim and reassign the lead to yourself in Salesforce — and once claimed, ignore territory boundaries entirely and work it immediately.

"Hi [Name], I noticed you were recently checking out our resources regarding [Specific Marketing Topic/Asset]. A lot of [Job Titles] I speak with who look into this are trying to bridge the gap between their field operations and office SaaS metrics. I'd love to sync for 10 minutes to show you how we helped [Similar Peer Company] optimize their workflow in this exact area…"

MPL rules of engagement:

  • Claim before you contact — you can't log activity or start outreach until the lead is officially reassigned to you in Salesforce.
  • The 24-hour clock — documented outreach (call, email, LinkedIn touch, or a Gong Flow) must start within 1 business day of assignment.
  • Use it or lose it (the 3-day rule) — if an MPL sits 3 consecutive calendar days with no documented Salesforce activity, protection is automatically forfeited and it's open season for a teammate to claim.
  • Ignore territory borders — marketing data isn't always aligned to patches; pursue and book meetings from any MPL assigned to you regardless of geography, vertical, or account ownership. Speed-to-lead overrides territory rules, and you get full credit for the opportunity.
Turn your peers' forgotten leads into your pipeline. Routinely audit Salesforce for inactive, unworked MPLs that have crossed the 3-day threshold — if abandoned, reassign to yourself, log your first touch, and pick up a warm opportunity someone else left on the table.

23Metrics

Metrics aren't a number to hit — they're signals about what's actually working. The best SDRs don't ask "how do I hit my activity goal," they ask "which activities create the highest-quality conversations with the right customers." A hundred generic emails will almost always produce fewer real opportunities than twenty-five highly personalized conversations with strategic accounts — so the goal was never maximum activity. It's the right activity, with the right accounts, using the right message, at the right time.

Five Categories

Outbound performance breaks down into activity (what's being done), engagement (are prospects responding), conversion (are activities becoming conversations and opportunities), pipeline impact (is any of this contributing to revenue), and quality (are we creating the right opportunities, not just more of them). Activity is the input; the other four determine whether it actually turns into anything.

An SQO (Sales Qualified Opportunity) is the next stage: an opportunity that's passed the AE's own qualification standards and is actively progressing through the sales process. The SAO → SQO conversion rate is one of the most useful diagnostics available — a high SAO volume with a low SQO rate almost always points back to qualification quality, not effort.

Pipeline & ACV Contribution

Pipeline contribution is the potential revenue tied to opportunities you've created or influenced — the actual link between your daily activity and future business impact. ACV (Annual Contract Value) contribution weights that further: not every opportunity is worth the same, and an SDR working Enterprise accounts should weigh account value and strategic importance, not just count.

Territory penetration — the percentage of target accounts actually contacted, and how many have active engagement — is the coverage check on the account prioritization work in Sections 7 and 8. A territory can't generate pipeline if priority accounts are sitting untouched. Account coverage is the same idea one level deeper: for Mid-Market and Enterprise accounts specifically, one contact is rarely enough (Section 9's multi-threading guidance applies directly here) — strong coverage means decision-makers, influencers, users, and technical stakeholders, not just whoever answered first.

Quality, Underneath the Numbers

Quantity creates opportunities; quality creates pipeline. Worth checking regularly: does the account actually fit the ICP, are you engaging the right personas, is there a real business challenge behind the activity, is the prospect actively participating rather than just responding politely, and is the opportunity continuing to move rather than stalling.

Review Rhythm

Daily: completed activities, priority follow-ups, engagement from the day. Weekly: conversations created, meetings booked, account progress, pipeline impact. Monthly: conversion trends, territory coverage, and whether messaging needs to adjust. Each cadence answers a different question — daily is "did I execute," weekly is "is it working," monthly is "what should change."

24Performance Excellence

Great SDRs aren't created by activity alone — they're created by continuous improvement. Average performers wait for feedback and ask what their manager is looking for. High performers seek feedback out and ask what they can do to get better. The goal was never perfection. It's progress, applied consistently.

The Growth Loop

Execute (complete the activities, engage the customer), measure (review the actual results), reflect (understand what worked and what didn't), adjust (change the behavior, message, or strategy), and repeat. It's a simple cycle, but most of the value is in actually closing the loop — skipping the reflect step is the most common way this breaks down.

Self-Coaching

The ability to evaluate your own performance is one of the strongest predictors of long-term success in this role, mostly because you spend far more time executing independently than you do with your manager. The shift is small but real: instead of asking "did this work," ask "what did I learn." After a call, email, or meeting, three questions are usually enough — what went well, what could improve, and what will I actually do differently next time. A few sharper prompts by category: on messaging, did it focus on the customer's priorities and create real curiosity? On conversations, did I listen more than I talked, and did I actually uncover a business challenge? On execution, did I prioritize the right accounts and follow up on what I said I would?

Reviewing Your Own Calls

Section 20 covers what Gong is for at a high level; this is how to actually use it for self-improvement. The point of reviewing your own calls isn't to criticize yourself — it's to find specific, fixable moments. Listen for the opening (did it create interest quickly and land as relevant), the flow (strong questions, or a slide into pitching too early), customer engagement (did they participate, or did you carry the whole conversation), and the next step (was it clear, and did the customer have a reason to want it).

A useful documentation format after a review: what worked (specifically — "the industry-specific opening created engagement because the prospect immediately recognized the challenge"), one opportunity to improve ("I could have asked more about the impact of the current process"), and one concrete action ("in my next five calls, I'll ask at least three discovery questions before mentioning a solution"). Vague takeaways don't change behavior; specific ones do.

Inspecting Your Own Pipeline

Pipeline health isn't only an AE's job to track. Section 23 covers the metrics themselves; the questions worth asking yourself regularly are about the story behind them — are you engaging the right accounts and reaching enough stakeholders within them, is there a real business challenge and genuine customer engagement behind what's in your pipeline, and are conversations actually progressing into meetings, SAOs, and beyond, or stalling at a predictable point.

Reading Your Scorecard

Section 23 has the full scorecard structure. Beyond "did I hit my number," it's worth asking what behaviors actually drove the good weeks, what patterns show up across your data, where your biggest single opportunity for improvement sits, and what one skill would move the needle most if you focused on it next.

Where to Keep Improving

A 1% improvement compounds fast when it's daily rather than occasional. Worth developing deliberately: prospecting skills (research, messaging, personalization), communication (listening, questioning, confidence), business acumen (industry knowledge, market awareness), and core sales skills (qualification, discovery, objection handling).

Building a Development Plan

A plan worth keeping covers four things: your actual strengths (what you're already doing well), the growth area that would move the needle most, specific actions (reviewing five Gong calls, shadowing an AE discovery call, practicing a specific messaging framework, completing a training module), and how you'll know it worked (a higher conversion rate, better conversations, more confidence — something you can actually observe).

Getting the Most From Manager Coaching

Coaching works best as a two-way conversation, not a one-way review. Managers typically coach across three areas: strategy (territory planning, account prioritization), skill (messaging, calls, discovery, objections), and performance (metrics, behaviors, improvement plans). It goes further when you show up prepared — bring actual data ("my connect rate has dropped over the last month"), a real observation ("prospects seem to engage more when I lead with industry challenges"), and a specific question ("what would you change about my opening?") rather than waiting to be told what to work on.

The Underlying Structure

Current state ("What are you doing today?") → challenges ("What isn't working as well as you'd like?") → impact ("How does that affect your business?") → future state ("What would you like to improve?") → next step ("Would it make sense to explore how others are addressing this?"). Coaching tip, general: never position the customer's current solution or process as wrong — the conversation should be about business change and future needs, not a verdict on their past decisions.

Two More Objections

"We're happy with our current process." The customer may genuinely not see a reason to change.

"That's great to hear. A lot of organizations we work with were also comfortable with their process, but started exploring improvements as projects became more complex or teams grew. What's been the biggest factor in making your current process successful?"

Follow-ups: "What would you change if you could improve one thing?" "Where do you see the biggest opportunities for efficiency?"

"We don't have budget." Often means no current initiative, or the value isn't clear yet — not a hard no.

"I understand, budget's always a factor. Usually teams evaluate solutions once they see a meaningful opportunity to improve something. Before we talk investment, it may help to understand whether there's a business challenge here worth solving at all."

Don't jump to discussing pricing or discounts — establish the problem and its impact first.

General Principles

Listen completely instead of preparing your response while they're still talking. Stay calm — an objection isn't rejection. Personalize the response back to their industry, role, and specific challenge. And know when to move on: professional persistence includes recognizing when a prospect genuinely isn't ready, and stepping back gracefully.
